# Assertion–Reason (real UPSC naming: Statement-I / Statement-II)

Confirmed from real papers — Tier 2, rising sharply since 2025, see
`../format-evolution.md`. UPSC doesn't actually use the labels "Assertion"
and "Reason" — the real convention is **Statement-I** and **Statement-II**,
with option wording built around whether Statement-II *explains*
Statement-I. Use that exact naming; "Assertion/Reason" will read as
off-pattern to anyone who's seen a real paper.

## Structure and components

Two linked statements:

- **Statement-I**: a claim.
- **Statement-II**: a second claim that may or may not correctly *explain*
  Statement-I.

Two separate judgments happen at once — is each statement true on its own,
and, if both are true, does Statement-II actually explain Statement-I
(versus being a true-but-unrelated fact sitting next to it). That second
judgment is what makes this inherently more conceptual than a simple
true/false check: both facts can be individually correct and the question
can still turn on whether one causes/explains the other.

Standard option template — keep this fixed, it's part of what makes the
format recognisable:

```
(a) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct, and Statement-II is the
    correct explanation for Statement-I
(b) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct, and Statement-II is NOT
    the correct explanation for Statement-I
(c) Statement-I is correct, but Statement-II is incorrect
(d) Statement-I is incorrect, but Statement-II is correct
```

## Depth

Almost never pure recall. Even when Statement-I and Statement-II are both
individually simple facts, judging the explanatory link requires
understanding *why* something is true, not just *that* it's true. Reach for
this over plain statement-based when the content has a clear cause → effect
or definition → implication relationship worth isolating — Economy,
Environment, Science & Tech, and Geography's physical-process questions
are natural homes for it (see `../subject-coverage.md`).

## Scope — selecting Statement-I and Statement-II

Pick a genuine causal, definitional, or mechanistic relationship — not two
unrelated true facts bolted together (makes option (b) trivially obvious),
and not a relationship so tight that Statement-II just rewords Statement-I
(makes option (a) trivially obvious). The sweet spot, from the confirmed
examples: Statement-II is a real, verifiable mechanism or precondition, and
Statement-I is a real, verifiable outcome, and the reader has to actually
know *why* to connect them — e.g. "India depends on coal despite having
uranium deposits" (I) paired against "uranium must be enriched to 60%+ for
power generation" (II): both individually checkable facts, and the
explanatory link is the real test (here, II turns out not to explain I,
since power-grade enrichment is far lower than that).

## Skeleton

```
Consider the following statements:
Statement-I: <claim>
Statement-II: <claim that may explain Statement-I>

Which one of the following is correct in respect of the above statements?
(a) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct, and Statement-II is the
    correct explanation for Statement-I
(b) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct, and Statement-II is NOT
    the correct explanation for Statement-I
(c) Statement-I is correct, but Statement-II is incorrect
(d) Statement-I is incorrect, but Statement-II is correct

Answer: (x)

Explanation:
Answer is (x).

Statement-I is <true/false>. <justification>.
Statement-II is <true/false>. <justification>.
[If both true: explicitly judge whether Statement-II actually causes/
explains Statement-I, and if not, name what Statement-II is actually a
fact about — the trap is usually that it sounds relevant without being the
real cause.]

<Self-contained paragraph on the underlying concept/mechanism.>
```

## Variety reminders

- Don't let the answer always land on (a) or (c) — genuinely vary which of
  the four holds across a batch/session.
- Vary whether the trap is a false Statement-I, a false Statement-II, or a
  true Statement-II that doesn't actually explain Statement-I — don't
  always use the same failure mode.
