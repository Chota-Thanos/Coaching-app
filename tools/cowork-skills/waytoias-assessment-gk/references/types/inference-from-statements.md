# Inference-from-statements

Confirmed from the 2026 paper (Q18, Ancient History — Harappan archaeology).
Closely related to `assertion-with-basis.md` and
`relationship-among-statements.md`, but the reasoning direction differs:
here the **evidence is given and the inferences are judged**.

## Structure and components

Two layers, using the 2026 numbering convention (see
`../format-evolution.md`):

- **Roman numerals** — the base evidence. Factual findings, presented as
  given, not as things to fact-check.
- **Arabic numerals** — candidate inferences, each explicitly citing a base
  statement by label: "Statement I suggests that…", "Statement III suggests
  the emergence of…".

The task is to judge whether each inference **validly follows** from the
evidence it cites — not whether the evidence is true, and not whether the
inference is independently plausible.

```
Which of the following inferences can be drawn from the above statements?
```

Options are combinations of the inference numbers.

## Depth

High, and of a specific kind: this tests whether a reader can distinguish
*what evidence actually shows* from *what sounds like a reasonable thing to
believe about the topic*. The characteristic trap is an inference that is
perfectly plausible as a claim about the subject but overreaches what the
cited finding supports — in the 2026 item, spacious courtyards with private
wells and bathing platforms were offered as suggesting "the emergence of a
common property system", when private amenities point the other way.

This is closer to historiographical or scientific reasoning than to recall,
which is why it lands naturally in archaeology, palaeo-environment, and
experimental-science material where conclusions are genuinely inferred from
traces.

## Scope — selecting evidence and inferences

Pick evidence that genuinely underdetermines its conclusions — findings that
support some readings and not others. The whole format depends on there
being real inferential distance between the trace and the claim.

For the candidate inferences, aim for a mix of: one that follows cleanly,
one that overreaches (plausible about the topic, unsupported by *this*
evidence), and optionally one that inverts what the evidence implies. Avoid
inferences that are simply restatements of the evidence in other words —
those are trivially "valid" and teach nothing.

Because the base statements are given as true, verify them properly
yourself: an inference question resting on a shaky finding is unfixable
downstream.

## Skeleton

```
Consider the following statements about <topic>:
I. <evidence>
II. <evidence>
III. <evidence>

Which of the following inferences can be drawn from the above statements?
1. Statement I suggests that <inference>.
2. Statement II suggests <inference>.
3. Statement III suggests <inference>.
(a) 1 and 2 only
(b) 2 and 3 only
(c) 1 and 3 only
(d) 1, 2 and 3

Answer: (x)

Explanation:
Answer is (x).

The base findings are each well attested: <brief confirmation of I, II, III>.

Inference 1 <does/does not> follow. <Explain the inferential step — what the
evidence establishes, and whether the claim stays within it.>
Inference 2 ...
Inference 3 ...
[For an inference that fails: state clearly that the claim may or may not be
true of the topic generally, but that THIS evidence doesn't establish it —
that distinction is the entire lesson of the format.]

<Self-contained paragraph on the topic and on how the evidence is read.>
```

## Variety reminders

- Rotate which inference fails, and vary the failure mode — overreach,
  inversion of what the evidence shows, or citing evidence that's simply
  irrelevant to the claim.
- Don't let every item have exactly one bad inference; sometimes two fail,
  and occasionally all three hold.
