# Scenario, situational-judgement and dialogue formats

New in the 2026 paper and genuinely novel for Prelims GS Paper 1: Q76, Q77,
Q78 (public administration / ethics scenarios) and Q79 (a two-speaker
dialogue on Article 13). This is GS Paper 4-style material appearing in
Paper 1 — worth writing for, since it signals a widened content brief, not
just a new wrapper.

Three related shapes.

## 1. Case-vignette → identify-the-principle

A short narrative about an official facing a situation, then a question
asking which named principle their conduct best demonstrates. Options are
bare concept names.

```
<Narrative: who, what pressure, what they did.>

Which one among the following principles of public administration was most
strongly demonstrated by <person>'s actions?
(a) Esprit de corps
(b) Equity
(c) Accountability
(d) Delegation
```

**Depth:** requires knowing the principles precisely enough to tell close
neighbours apart, and reading the narrative for which one the conduct
actually turns on. Write the vignette so exactly one principle is decisive —
if two fit equally, the item is unfair. The distractors should be principles
that *are* present in the story's background but aren't what the key action
demonstrates.

## 2. Scenario + candidate actions (situational judgement)

A longer scenario — typically a conflict with competing legitimate interests
— followed by numbered candidate propositions or courses of action, and a
prompt asking which would contribute to resolving it.

```
<Extended scenario: competing stakeholders, escalation, your role.>
1. <proposition or action>
2. <proposition or action>
3. <action>
4. <action>

Which of the statements given above would contribute to the resolution
process?
```

**Depth and the key caution:** the truth criterion here is **desirability,
not factual correctness** — which makes this the format most at risk of
being merely opinion. Keep it defensible by building wrong options around
recognisable governance failures rather than debatable judgement calls:
overriding a community's concerns because they are a minority, acting
without consultation, suppressing information. The 2026 items followed this
— the one clearly wrong option was "move ahead without delay… sentiments of
a small group outweighed", which fails on established participatory-
governance grounds rather than on taste.

Note the 2026 items mixed grammatical forms across the numbered list
(propositions and gerund-phrase actions in the same set). It's not elegant,
but it is the observed pattern; prefer internal consistency unless you're
deliberately reproducing that.

## 3. Two-speaker dialogue adjudication

Two named speakers advance positions, one disputing the other; the options
adjudicate whose view holds.

```
'X' was addressing a seminar on <topic>. 'X' explained that <position>. 'Y'
pointed out that <competing or additional position>, to which 'X' was not
convinced.

Based on the above, select the correct conclusion from the options given
below:
(a) 'X' is correct in the interpretation, including the view on <the
    disputed element>.
(b) The view of 'Y' that <claim> is not correct.
(c) The views of both 'X' and 'Y' are correct.
(d) The view of only 'Y' is correct.
```

**Depth:** logically similar to Statement-I/II — two positions, each true or
false — but staged as disagreement, which adds a comprehension load: the
reader must first extract what each speaker actually committed to. Note that
in the 2026 item options (a) and (b) were near-equivalent restatements of
each other; that's a real feature of the observed question, but writing two
options that collapse into one is a flaw worth avoiding rather than copying.

Build these on genuine interpretive questions with a settled answer — a
constitutional definition, a statutory scope question — so the adjudication
is defensible rather than a matter of view.

## Explanation rules for all three

The standard structure still applies, adapted: state the answer, then work
through each candidate action / principle / speaker's position with real
justification, name the trap for the wrong ones, and close with a
self-contained paragraph on the underlying principle or provision.

For situational items, the closing paragraph should explain **why** the
sound actions are sound in governance terms — participatory decision-making,
transparency, proportionality, due process. Without that, the item reads as
the writer's preference rather than as teachable content.

## Variety reminders

- Vary the domain: administrative ethics, conflict mediation, disclosure
  dilemmas, constitutional interpretation.
- Don't make the correct set always "all except the obviously callous one" —
  that becomes a one-rule shortcut across a batch.
- Keep scenarios compact. The 2026 versions ran long, but length is not what
  creates the difficulty; the competing legitimate interests are.
